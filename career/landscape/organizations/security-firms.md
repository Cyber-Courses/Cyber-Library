# Security Firms

Security firms are companies whose primary business is cybersecurity, as opposed to general technology companies that happen to sell a security product on the side. The category spans product-focused vendors in areas such as endpoint detection, identity, and network defense, and service-focused providers that deliver penetration testing, managed detection and response, incident response, and threat intelligence. What unites them is that security is the whole enterprise rather than one division among many, which shapes their culture, their hiring, and the depth of expertise they concentrate in a given domain.

For a security career, these firms matter as some of the most specialized employers in the field. Because their revenue depends on security outcomes, they tend to invest heavily in practitioner skill, maintain dedicated research teams, and expose staff to a wide range of client environments and adversary behavior. A junior analyst at a managed detection provider may see more real incidents in a year than a counterpart in a single in-house team would see in several. That intensity makes security firms a common place to build credibility early in a career, and the names of well-regarded firms carry weight on a resume.

The day-to-day work varies by the firm's focus. In a service firm, practitioners often work in a billable or engagement-driven model, rotating across customer environments, writing reports, and defending findings to clients. In a product vendor, roles lean toward detection engineering, threat research, software development, and sales engineering that supports the product. Both settings reward deep technical skill, clear written and spoken communication, and the discipline to document work to a standard that others can rely on. People typically enter through analyst, researcher, or engineering roles and grow by specializing in a domain, moving into lead or principal positions, or shifting toward client-facing and management tracks.

Security firms sit close to their sibling organization types but occupy a distinct position. Technology firms build security capability as part of a broader platform, so roles there often blend security with general software or infrastructure work. Consultancies emphasize advisory relationships, governance, and program-level guidance rather than operating security services at scale. Standards bodies define the frameworks and expectations that all of these organizations work against. Many practitioners move among these settings over a career, and time at a focused security firm is frequently the foundation that makes those later moves possible.
