# Leadership and Management Opportunities

Leadership and management opportunities are the points in a security career where a professional moves from doing the work to shaping how the work gets done and guiding the people who do it. They take two broad forms. **Formal management** means holding a title with direct reports, budget, and accountability for a team's output, such as a security team lead, SOC manager, or director of security. **Technical or informal leadership** means setting direction and raising the bar without organizational authority, such as owning a vulnerability management program, serving as the senior voice in incident response, or acting as a trusted technical advisor across teams. Both are valued, and many organizations maintain parallel tracks so a strong practitioner can grow in seniority without being required to manage people.

## Why these roles exist

- Security is a team discipline where outcomes depend on coordination, judgment, and sustained priorities rather than individual heroics.
- As a program matures, someone has to decide where limited attention goes, translate risk into language executives understand, and develop the next generation of analysts and engineers.
- A professional who can do this multiplies the effectiveness of everyone around them, which is why leadership roles tend to carry more compensation and influence than purely individual contributions at the same experience level.

## How the day-to-day work changes

- A **manager** spends time setting priorities, running one-on-one conversations, giving feedback, hiring, handling performance issues, managing budgets, and representing the team to other departments.
- A **technical leader** spends time reviewing designs, mentoring, setting standards, resolving disagreements about approach, and making the case for security investments.
- In both cases, measurable hands-on time usually decreases, and the work becomes more about enabling others, communicating clearly, and owning results delivered through a group rather than alone.

## What the roles require

- Credibility with the team still rests on a solid security foundation, but the roles call for more: clear written and verbal communication, the ability to prioritize under uncertainty, comfort with difficult conversations, basic financial and planning literacy, and genuine interest in the growth of other people.
- People typically enter leadership by first demonstrating **informal influence**, such as reliably mentoring colleagues, coordinating a project end to end, or improving a shared process, and are then offered a lead role as the team grows or a vacancy opens.
- Growth from there follows the scope of responsibility, moving from leading a small team to running a function to setting strategy across an organization.

## How it relates to other growth

Leadership interacts closely with the other ways a career advances in place. Internal mobility can open a management opening when a team expands or reorganizes; performance feedback and reviews are often the mechanism through which readiness for leadership is recognized, and where a new leader learns to evaluate others; and continuous skill enhancement remains necessary so a leader keeps enough technical currency to earn the team's trust. Choosing leadership is a deliberate change in the nature of the work, not simply a promotion, and it rewards those who find satisfaction in building teams and programs rather than only in solving technical problems directly.
