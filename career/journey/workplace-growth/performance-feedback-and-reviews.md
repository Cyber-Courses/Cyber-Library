# Performance Feedback and Reviews

Performance feedback and reviews are the structured and informal mechanisms an organization uses to assess how well a security professional is doing their work and to guide what they do next. Feedback is the ongoing, often informal, exchange that happens throughout the year, while a review is the periodic formal checkpoint, commonly quarterly or annual, that summarizes contribution, sets expectations, and informs decisions about compensation, promotion, and development. Together they turn day-to-day effort into a shared record of impact and a plan for improvement.

This process matters in a security career because much security work is preventive and quiet, and its value is not always obvious from the outside. A quarter spent hardening systems, tuning detections, or closing out audit findings may prevent incidents that never make headlines. Reviews give a professional the chance to document that contribution in terms the organization recognizes, and feedback helps a practitioner understand whether their judgment, communication, and priorities are aligned with what the team and the business actually need.

The substance of a useful review rests on concrete evidence rather than general impressions. Strong examples include incidents handled well, measurable reductions in risk, projects delivered, mentoring provided, and the reliability with which routine responsibilities were met. Many organizations frame evaluation against defined competencies or a leveling rubric, so that expectations for a junior analyst differ clearly from those for a senior engineer. A professional who keeps a running record of accomplishments, metrics, and peer acknowledgments through the year makes both self-assessment and manager evaluation far more accurate.

Feedback is most valuable when it is specific, behavioral, and timely, and when it flows in more than one direction. Receiving feedback well means treating criticism as information rather than judgment and asking clarifying questions about what good performance would look like. Giving feedback, including upward to a manager or across to a peer, is itself a professional skill that grows in importance with seniority. Calibration, where managers compare ratings across a team to keep standards consistent, is a common part of this process and explains why individual outcomes depend partly on organizational norms rather than a single manager's view.

Approached deliberately, reviews become a planning tool rather than a verdict. They are the natural moment to agree on development goals, surface interest in new responsibilities, and discuss the path toward promotion or expanded scope. Because this work happens within an existing role and relationship, it complements rather than replaces the other ways a professional advances, and the record it builds is what later supports the case for a larger title or a different position.
