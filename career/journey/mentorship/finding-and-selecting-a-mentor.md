# Finding and Selecting a Mentor

Finding and selecting a mentor is the process by which a security practitioner identifies someone whose experience can inform their own path and then opens a relationship with that person. It is the entry point to mentorship, concerned less with what the relationship will accomplish and more with who the right guide might be and how to reach them. For a field as wide and fast-moving as cybersecurity, the choice of mentor shapes the quality of the guidance that follows, so it rewards deliberate thought rather than chance.

Good candidates rarely appear through a formal search. They emerge from relationships already forming at work, in professional communities, through conferences and local chapters, and across the networks a practitioner builds over time. A manager a few levels up, a respected colleague on an adjacent team, an active member of a security community, or a speaker whose work resonates can all become mentors. Many organizations and professional bodies also maintain mentoring programs that pair people deliberately, which can help those who lack an established network or who want guidance outside their own employer.

Selecting well means weighing fit as much as seniority. The most senior person is not always the best guide; what matters is relevant experience, a willingness to invest time, and a communication style the mentee can learn from. Someone who has navigated the specific transition a mentee faces, such as moving from a generalist role into a specialization or from a technical track into leadership, often offers more useful perspective than a broader but less relevant expert. Honesty, approachability, and genuine interest in the mentee's growth distinguish a productive relationship from a nominal one.

Approaching a prospective mentor works best as a modest, specific request rather than a sweeping ask for ongoing commitment. A concrete question about a decision, a field, or a piece of work invites a manageable response and lets both people gauge rapport before either commits to more. Many lasting mentorships begin this way and deepen naturally when the interest proves mutual. Being considerate of the mentor's time, prepared for conversations, and clear about what guidance is sought all make the relationship easier to sustain.

This stage sits at the front of the mentoring arc and connects closely to the work that follows. Once a mentor is found, the pair move on to establishing goals and expectations, may operate within a formal mentoring program or a looser arrangement, and ideally build a relationship that lasts beyond any single question. Selecting the right person first makes each of those later stages more productive.
