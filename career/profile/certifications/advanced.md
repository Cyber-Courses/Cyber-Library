# Advanced

At the top of most credentialing paths sits the advanced tier, aimed at practitioners who have moved well beyond foundational and intermediate material. These credentials are not an introduction to a subject. They assume years of hands-on work and ask the candidate to demonstrate either broad command of how security is **governed and designed** across an organization, or deep **technical mastery** that few people reach. Many carry formal experience requirements, peer endorsement, or demanding practical examinations, and several require ongoing education to remain valid.

## Two directions at the top

What this tier signals depends on which way a credential leans: toward leadership of a program, or toward solving hard technical problems under realistic conditions. In both cases the common message to an employer is **maturity**, the holder has seen enough to exercise judgment rather than only follow a procedure.

## Management and governance credentials

These indicate that a person can set strategy, manage risk, and lead a program rather than only operate tools:

- The **Certified Information Systems Security Professional (CISSP)** from (ISC)2.
- The **Certified Information Security Manager (CISM)** from ISACA.
- The **Certified Information Systems Auditor (CISA)** from ISACA.

## Expert technical credentials

These signal rigor and the ability to solve difficult problems:

- The **Offensive Security Certified Expert 3 (OSCE3)**, which replaced the retired OSCE and is earned by holding the OSED, OSEP, and OSWE certifications.
- The **Offensive Security Exploitation Expert (OSEE)**.
- The **GIAC Security Expert (GSE)**.

## Who it suits

Advanced certifications suit experienced practitioners who are consolidating a reputation or stepping into higher responsibility: senior engineers and consultants, security architects, team leads, and managers moving toward roles such as principal consultant or chief information security officer. The tier validates competence that is already substantial, which is why holders usually pursue these credentials several years into a career rather than at its start. It helps to approach them once entry-level and intermediate groundwork is real rather than rushed. A career often pairs an advanced credential, which proves seniority and breadth, with a specialized one, which proves domain depth.
