# Threat Intelligence

Threat intelligence is the discipline of studying adversaries, the campaigns they run, and the methods they favor, then turning that understanding into guidance that helps an organization defend itself. A threat intelligence analyst answers questions that purely technical signals cannot: who is likely to target this organization, what they tend to go after, how they typically operate, and what that means for where defenses should be strengthened first. The work matters to a security career because it supplies the context that makes everything else more deliberate. Detection rules, patching priorities, and executive decisions all improve when they are informed by a clear picture of the relevant threats rather than by reaction alone.

The practice is often described across three levels that shape the day-to-day work. Strategic intelligence looks at long-horizon trends, motivations, and geopolitical factors, and it speaks to leadership about risk. Operational intelligence tracks specific campaigns and the groups behind them, following how their behavior changes over time. Tactical intelligence concerns the concrete and often short-lived technical details, such as indicators tied to a particular intrusion, that defenders and detection engineers can act on quickly. A mature program produces all three and knows which audience each one serves.

Day to day, an analyst gathers information from a wide range of sources, which may include internal telemetry, commercial and open feeds, information-sharing communities, and reporting on known adversary groups. The core skill is analysis rather than collection: weighing the reliability of sources, separating confirmed fact from assessment, mapping observed behavior to recognized frameworks, and stating conclusions with an honest sense of confidence. The output is a product, typically a written assessment, a briefing, or a structured set of indicators, that reaches a specific audience and answers a defined question. Because that product only has value when people use it, clear writing and well-judged communication are as important as technical depth.

The role calls for analytical rigor, curiosity, and strong writing, supported by a working knowledge of how networks, systems, and common attack behaviors fit together. Familiarity with structured analytic methods, with frameworks that catalog adversary tactics and techniques, and with the discipline of avoiding bias all help an analyst reach sound judgments. People commonly enter from backgrounds in security operations, incident response, or general intelligence analysis, and some arrive from research, journalism, or military and government settings where assessment and reporting are already second nature. With experience, an analyst may specialize in tracking particular adversaries or regions, lead an intelligence function, or move toward threat hunting and detection engineering.

Threat intelligence sits close to several neighboring roles without duplicating them. It feeds the investigative work of DFIR by providing context on who and what an organization is facing, and it guides defensive security teams on what to watch for and prioritize, while leaving detection engineering and response operations to those functions. Its contribution is the analysis and communication of adversary knowledge, which lets the rest of the security program act with foresight rather than only in reaction.
