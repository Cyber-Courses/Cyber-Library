# IAM

Identity and Access Management, usually shortened to IAM, is the security specialization concerned with establishing who and what can access an organization's systems, and with ensuring that each identity has only the access it genuinely needs. It covers the full life cycle of an identity, from the moment an account is created through every change in role to the point it is removed, and it spans the people, services, and devices that interact with modern environments. As organizations have moved toward cloud platforms, remote work, and interconnected applications, identity has become a primary security boundary, which places IAM at the center of how access is granted, proven, and revoked.

Day to day, practitioners in this field design and operate the controls that authenticate users and authorize their actions. The work includes administering directory services and single sign-on, enforcing multi-factor authentication, defining roles and entitlements so that permissions map cleanly to job function, and running the provisioning and deprovisioning processes that keep access current as people join, move, and leave. A recurring theme is the principle of least privilege, pursued through access reviews, certification campaigns, and the steady reduction of standing and excessive permissions. Privileged access management, which protects administrative and high-risk accounts, and the governance of non-human identities such as service accounts and application credentials are growing parts of the remit. Much of the value lies in making access both reliable for legitimate users and difficult to misuse.

The role draws on a blend of technical and organizational skill. Practitioners need a solid grasp of authentication and authorization concepts, familiarity with protocols and standards such as SAML, OAuth, OpenID Connect, LDAP, and SCIM, and working knowledge of directory and identity platforms across on-premises and cloud settings. Equally important is fluency with the business: IAM touches nearly every department, so the ability to translate access policy into practical role designs, to collaborate with application owners and auditors, and to document decisions clearly often matters as much as configuration skill. Scripting and automation are valuable because identity processes are repetitive and benefit greatly from consistency.

People commonly enter IAM from system administration, help desk and directory support, cloud administration, or a general security operations background, bringing an understanding of how accounts and permissions behave in real environments. Early work tends to be operational, handling access requests, account life cycle tasks, and support, before growing into engineering roles that build integrations and automate provisioning, and then into governance and architecture roles that define access models and policy across the organization. Senior paths lead toward identity architecture, program ownership, or leadership of an identity function. The specialization sits close to Security Architecture, which sets the broader design that IAM implements, and to GRC, which relies on IAM evidence for access certifications and audits; it also supports Cloud Security, where identity is often the main control plane.
