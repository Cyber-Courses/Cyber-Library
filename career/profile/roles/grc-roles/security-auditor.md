# Security Auditor

A security auditor provides independent assurance that an organization's security controls exist, are well designed, and actually work as intended. Where many security roles build or defend systems, the auditor examines them from a deliberate arm's length, testing whether the safeguards an organization claims to have are real and effective. The work is anchored in a framework or standard such as **ISO/IEC 27001**, the **NIST frameworks**, **SOC 2** criteria, **PCI DSS**, or a sector regulation, and the auditor's judgments are expressed as findings backed by evidence. That independence is what gives an audit its value to boards, regulators, customers, and business partners.

## Responsibilities

- Scope an engagement, identify the controls in play, and request evidence: configurations, access lists, change tickets, logs, policies, and records of prior reviews.
- Test controls by inspecting documents, reinspecting system settings, reperforming a control, observing a process, or interviewing the people who run it.
- Compare findings against the applicable criteria and write up each gap with its condition, the criterion it fails, the underlying cause, and a recommendation, rated by severity.
- Sample sensibly and document work so another professional could follow it.
- Track remediation and often retest before closing items.

## Skills and background

- **Technical literacy:** working knowledge of operating systems, networks, identity and access management, cloud platforms, and application controls, enough to ask sharp questions and recognize a weak answer.
- **Professional skepticism:** the discipline to distinguish a genuine control weakness from a cosmetic one.
- **Methodical documentation:** clear writing and work papers that stand on their own.
- **Composure:** the steadiness to deliver uncomfortable findings and defend them calmly.

## Getting into the role

People commonly enter as junior or staff auditors in an internal audit function, at an external audit or assurance firm, or through a consultancy, learning the methodology on real engagements before leading their own. A background in accounting-style internal audit, IT operations, or compliance is common. Many auditors hold certifications such as ISACA's CISA, and some add the CIA or an ISO 27001 lead auditor credential.

## Career progression

Growth runs toward senior auditor, audit manager, and audit director or chief audit executive, with specialization available in areas like cloud, privacy, or a regulated industry. Because the work exposes someone to controls across the whole organization, it is also a strong platform for moving into risk, compliance, or security leadership later.
