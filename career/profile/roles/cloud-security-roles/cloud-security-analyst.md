# Cloud Security Analyst

A cloud security analyst is a security professional who watches over an organization's cloud environments and judges how well they are protected. The work centers on assessing configuration, access, and activity across platforms such as Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, and Google Cloud, then translating what the platform reports into a clear picture of risk. The role is more observational and analytical than it is hands-on with building infrastructure, and it exists because cloud accounts generate a continuous stream of signals that need informed interpretation before they become useful to a security program.

Day to day, the analyst reviews the security posture of cloud accounts and the resources running inside them, looking for misconfigurations, overly broad permissions, exposed storage, unencrypted data, and gaps against internal baselines. They monitor logs and alerts from native services and from cloud security posture management and workload protection tools, triage what those alerts mean, and separate genuine concerns from noise. When something warrants attention, the analyst investigates the context, documents the finding, rates its severity, and routes it to the team that can fix it. A large part of the value lies in the write-up: a well-framed finding explains what is wrong, why it matters, and what a reasonable remediation looks like, so that engineers and owners can act without guesswork.

The role calls for a solid grasp of how cloud platforms are structured, including identity and access management, networking, storage, and logging, together with an understanding of shared responsibility and common control frameworks. Analysts benefit from familiarity with at least one major provider, comfort reading logs and querying data, and a working knowledge of how attacks against cloud resources tend to unfold, so they can recognize meaningful patterns. Equally important are the habits that make findings trustworthy: careful verification, consistent documentation, sound prioritization, and communication that reaches both technical staff and the people accountable for a service.

People commonly enter this role from security operations, help desk or system administration, network roles, or general IT, often after adding cloud fundamentals and a provider certification to an existing security foundation. Early work tends to emphasize monitoring and triage, and with experience an analyst takes on more complex assessments, tuning of detection logic, and ownership of posture across larger estates. From there, some deepen their defensive focus and move toward cloud security operations or detection engineering, while others shift toward building and hardening systems or toward governance work. The role sits close to several neighbors: the cloud security engineer implements and automates the controls an analyst evaluates, the cloud security architect designs the overall security model, the cloud compliance specialist maps findings to regulatory and audit requirements, and the DevSecOps engineer embeds security checks into the delivery pipeline. The analyst's distinct contribution is sustained visibility: turning the ongoing stream of cloud signals into prioritized, actionable risk that the rest of the program can address.
