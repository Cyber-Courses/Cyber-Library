# Security Architect

A security architect decides how **security is built into** an organization's systems, applications, networks, and platforms. Instead of operating individual controls or responding to incidents, the architect works at the level of **design**, arranging components so that confidentiality, integrity, and availability hold by structure rather than by patching after the fact. The role sits where engineering meets governance, and it is one of the clearest routes into high-trust technical leadership whose decisions shape a whole program.

## Responsibilities

- Review proposed designs and produce the reference blueprints and secure design patterns that development and infrastructure teams reuse.
- Run **threat modeling** on new systems to anticipate how they could be abused.
- Select and specify controls such as identity and access management, encryption, network segmentation, and logging.
- Evaluate vendor products and cloud services for security fit, and define security requirements for projects.
- Advise on trade-offs when business goals and security pull in different directions, defending recommendations with reasoning that holds up under scrutiny.
- Write standards and explain decisions to both engineers and executives.

## Skills and background

- **Technical foundations:** a strong grounding in networking, operating systems, identity, cryptography, and cloud platforms, plus an understanding of how applications are built and deployed.
- **Risk reasoning:** the ability to map controls to frameworks and regulations and to think in terms of systems and dependencies rather than isolated tools.
- **Soft skills:** influence over teams the architect does not manage, and the persuasion to win acceptance for designs that trade convenience for resilience.
- **Credentials:** CISSP, SANS or cloud-provider architecture credentials, and frameworks such as the NIST publications and SABSA appear often, though demonstrated judgment matters more than any single one.

## Getting into the role

Most people reach this role after several years in hands-on security or infrastructure work. Common paths run through security engineering, systems or network administration, software development, or roles like penetration testing and incident response that build an intuition for how systems fail. Early in the architecture track, practitioners often focus on a single domain or a set of projects before taking on broader scope.

## Career progression

Growth typically moves toward wider responsibility: leading the architecture for a business unit, setting enterprise-wide standards, or progressing into principal architect, head of security architecture, or management and advisory positions that shape strategy. At larger organizations the function splits by scope, with enterprise and domain-focused architects owning different layers.
