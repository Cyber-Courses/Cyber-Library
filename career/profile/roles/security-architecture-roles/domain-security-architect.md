# Domain Security Architect

A domain security architect owns the design, standards, and control posture for **one defined slice** of an organization rather than for the enterprise as a whole. That slice may be a technology area such as cloud, identity, network, or data, a business function such as payments or manufacturing, or a single product line or platform. The role exists because large programs cannot be covered in sufficient depth by generalists alone: **domain depth** is what keeps architecture decisions accurate and defensible as systems grow more specialized.

## Responsibilities

- Produce and maintain **reference architectures** and security patterns for the domain.
- Define the control requirements that projects in the domain must satisfy.
- Review proposed designs for alignment with policy, regulatory obligations, and the organization's risk appetite.
- Serve as approval authority or a key reviewer on an architecture review board for anything touching the domain.
- Partner with engineering teams, product owners, and risk and compliance functions.
- Make trade-offs explicit, weighing a control's cost and friction against the risk it reduces, and documenting the rationale so decisions remain clear later.

## Skills and background

- **Domain depth:** genuine expertise in at least one area, since credibility rests on understanding how the domain's systems are actually built and operated.
- **Security fundamentals:** threat modeling, cryptography, identity and access management, secure network design, and the relevant compliance frameworks.
- **Communication:** strong written skills, because reference architectures and standards are durable artifacts others rely on without the author present, plus the ability to reason about risk in business terms.
- **Credentials:** CISSP and its architecture concentration ISSAP, SABSA for enterprise and domain method, TOGAF for broader architecture practice, and domain-specific certifications such as cloud provider security specialties.

## Getting into the role

Most people arrive after several years in a security engineering, infrastructure, or application security position, where they built the practical depth the title assumes. A common path runs from engineer to senior engineer to domain architect, with the transition marked by a shift from building and operating controls to defining how they should be built across many teams.

## Career progression

From here, architects often broaden toward the enterprise architect role, which coordinates across domains and sets program-wide direction, or deepen further into a principal or distinguished specialist track within their domain. Some move toward security leadership once they have shown they can align architecture with organizational strategy.
