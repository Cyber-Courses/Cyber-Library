# Privacy and Data Protection Roles

Privacy and data protection roles share a common purpose: to help an organization collect, use, and retain personal data responsibly, in keeping with applicable law and the reasonable expectations of the people that data describes. These professions sit at the intersection of law, engineering, risk management, and operations. Their value lies in translating broad legal obligations, such as those found in the GDPR or comparable regimes, into concrete controls, decisions, and habits that hold up in day-to-day practice.

Although the roles are related, they differ in emphasis, scope, and the kind of expertise they draw on. Some are primarily legal and advisory, some are hands-on and technical, and others coordinate the program that ties the work together. The pages beneath this section describe each role in turn, including its typical responsibilities, the skills it relies on, and how it fits alongside security, legal, and governance functions.

The [Data Protection Officer](data-protection-officer.md) holds a defined oversight and advisory position, monitoring compliance, serving as a point of contact for regulators and individuals, and advising leadership on data protection obligations. The [Privacy Analyst](privacy-analyst.md) supports this work in practice, conducting assessments, maintaining records of processing, and reviewing how data flows through systems and vendors. The [Privacy Program Manager](privacy-program-manager.md) organizes these activities into a coherent program, tracking commitments, coordinating stakeholders, and keeping policies, training, and reporting on schedule.

Two roles sit closer to the specialized disciplines they draw from. The [Privacy Engineer](privacy-engineer.md) embeds privacy into the design of systems and products, applying techniques such as data minimization, de-identification, and access control so that protection is built in rather than added later. The [Privacy Counsel](privacy-counsel.md) provides legal interpretation and judgment, advising on regulatory requirements, contracts, and emerging obligations, and guiding how the organization responds when questions of law arise.

Careers in this area rarely follow a single path. Practitioners often arrive from law, compliance, security, or software backgrounds, and many move between advisory, technical, and program roles over time. Reading the individual pages alongside this overview helps a professional see where their interests fit and how these roles complement one another and the wider effort to protect personal data.
