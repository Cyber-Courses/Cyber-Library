# IAM Engineer

Building and operating the technical systems that govern digital identity is the core of the IAM engineer's work. The engineer occupies the hands-on middle ground between the analyst who handles daily requests and the architect who sets long-range direction: translating access policy into working configuration, integrating applications with identity providers, and keeping authentication and authorization services running reliably. The role spans security, infrastructure, and software, and it matters because identity has become the primary **control plane** for environments where networks dissolve into cloud and remote work.

## Responsibilities

- Configure and maintain identity providers and directory services, and implement **single sign-on** and **multifactor authentication**.
- Connect business applications to central identity through standards such as SAML, OAuth 2.0, OpenID Connect, and SCIM.
- Automate provisioning and deprovisioning so access follows the employee lifecycle, building connectors between HR systems and directories and codifying role definitions.
- Troubleshoot login failures and federation issues, tune directory performance, and respond to access-related incidents.
- Support audits by producing evidence that controls operate as intended.
- Extend identity to machine and workload identity: service accounts, API credentials, certificates, and application secrets.

## Skills and background

- **Systems foundations:** directory technologies such as Active Directory and LDAP, cloud identity platforms from the major providers, and at least one major IAM product line.
- **Protocol depth:** the ability to reason about federation protocols rather than merely enabling them in a console.
- **Automation:** scripting in a language such as Python or PowerShell to automate repetitive provisioning and reporting tasks.
- **Supporting knowledge:** a working grasp of networking, public key infrastructure, and the principle of **least privilege**.
- **Collaboration:** clear configuration documentation and partnership with the application teams who own the systems being integrated.

## Getting into the role

Most people enter from an adjacent technical position rather than starting here directly. A common path runs from help desk or systems administration into an IAM analyst role, then into engineering as the person takes on more integration and automation work; others arrive from software development or cloud infrastructure and pick up the identity specialization along the way. Vendor credentials from identity platforms, complemented by broader security certifications, help structure the learning and signal competence.

## Career progression

Growth tends to move toward IAM architect for those drawn to design and strategy, toward privileged access management or access governance for those who want to specialize, or into security engineering and platform leadership more broadly. Because identity underpins so many other controls, the experience transfers well and demand for it has stayed steady.

## Related roles

- [IAM Analyst](iam-analyst.md): reviews and fulfills access requests and supports users, where the engineer builds the automation that scales those processes.
- [IAM Architect](iam-architect.md): defines the target state, standards, and technology selections the engineer implements and runs.
- [PAM Specialist](pam-specialist.md): concentrates on controls around administrative and high-risk accounts.
- [Access Governance Specialist](access-governance-specialist.md): owns the certification, policy, and compliance side of entitlements.
