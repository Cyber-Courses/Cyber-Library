# IAM Analyst

An IAM Analyst keeps an organization's identity and access management program running day to day. Where an IAM Engineer builds and automates the platform and an IAM Architect sets its direction, the analyst is the hands-on operator who administers user accounts, processes access requests, and ensures that the right people hold the right entitlements for the right reasons. The role sits at the intersection of security, IT operations, and the business, and it is often the most common entry point into an identity career. Because identity has become the primary control plane for modern security, the quality of this routine work has direct consequences for an organization's overall exposure.

The day-to-day work centers on the identity lifecycle, frequently described as the joiner, mover, and leaver process. Analysts create accounts for new hires, adjust entitlements when people change roles, and disable or remove access promptly when someone departs. They handle access requests through a ticketing or governance platform, confirming that approvals are in place and that grants align with least privilege. A large share of the job involves periodic access reviews and certifications, where the analyst gathers entitlement data, coordinates with managers and application owners to confirm what each person should retain, and documents the results for audit. Analysts also triage access-related issues, support authentication and single sign-on problems, maintain group and role definitions, and produce reports that demonstrate the program is working as intended.

The role calls for a blend of technical fluency and operational discipline. An analyst should understand core concepts such as authentication versus authorization, role-based and attribute-based access control, directory services like Active Directory and LDAP, and federation standards including SAML, OAuth, and OpenID Connect. Familiarity with an identity platform such as Okta, Microsoft Entra ID, SailPoint, or Ping is valuable, as is comfort working in spreadsheets and reporting tools to analyze large sets of entitlement data. Equally important are the softer traits the work demands: attention to detail, careful documentation, and the communication skills to coordinate across security, help desk, and business teams. An awareness of compliance drivers such as SOX, HIPAA, or ISO 27001 helps an analyst understand why access controls and evidence matter.

People commonly enter the role from a help desk, system administration, or general IT support background, and some arrive directly from a degree or an entry-level security certification. Foundational credentials such as CompTIA Security+ or vendor-specific certifications from the identity platform in use are typical early milestones, and broader governance certifications from ISACA or (ISC)2 become relevant as responsibilities grow. With experience, an analyst can specialize and advance along several paths. Deepening automation and platform skills leads toward the IAM Engineer role, a focus on policy, certification, and audit leads toward an Access Governance Specialist, and concentrating on high-risk credentials leads toward a PAM Specialist. Analysts who develop a broad view of identity strategy may eventually move toward architecture. The role is a durable foundation because the lifecycle, review, and least-privilege practices it teaches apply across every neighboring identity specialization.
