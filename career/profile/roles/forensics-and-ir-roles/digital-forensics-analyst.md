# Digital Forensics Analyst

A **Digital Forensics Analyst** recovers, preserves, and examines digital evidence to reconstruct what happened on a system, a device, or a network. The work answers factual questions: how an intrusion began, which accounts or files were touched, when actions occurred, and whether data left the organization. Because findings may support internal decisions, disciplinary matters, or legal proceedings, the role is defined as much by **rigor and defensibility** as by technical skill, so the analyst must show not only the conclusion but that the evidence was handled in a way that preserves its integrity.

## Responsibilities

- Create verified copies of disks, memory, mobile devices, and logs, documenting each step so the original evidence is never altered.
- Maintain a clear **chain of custody** from acquisition through analysis and storage.
- Reconstruct a **timeline** by examining file systems, operating system artifacts, application traces, and network records.
- Separate what the evidence supports from what it does not, documenting uncertainty honestly rather than overstating a conclusion.
- Write reports, and sometimes give testimony, that explain findings in plain, accurate terms for non-technical readers.
- Keep precise, reproducible notes so another examiner can follow the same path to the same result.

## Skills and background

- **Technical foundations:** how operating systems, file systems, and common applications store and leave behind data.
- **Tooling:** familiarity with established forensic tools and the artifacts they interpret.
- **Evidentiary discipline:** awareness of evidentiary standards, chain of custody, and the limits of what a given artifact can prove, which is what distinguishes forensic work from ordinary troubleshooting.
- **Temperament:** patience, skepticism, and the habit of documenting carefully.
- **Communication:** clear writing, since a report a non-technical reader cannot follow undermines the analysis.

## Getting into the role

Analysts commonly arrive from security operations, system administration, incident handling, or law enforcement, bringing an understanding of how real systems behave under normal and abnormal conditions. Many build credibility through structured study, hands-on practice with evidence handling, and recognized certifications before taking on sensitive cases.

## Career progression

With experience, an analyst may specialize in areas such as mobile, cloud, or memory forensics and take on more complex or higher-stakes investigations. From there the path leads toward lead examiner responsibilities that include mentoring and quality review, and eventually toward managing the function.

## Related roles

- [Incident Responder](incident-responder.md): contains and recovers from active threats under time pressure, where the analyst emphasizes thorough, defensible reconstruction after the fact.
- [Malware Reverse Engineer](malware-reverse-engineer.md): takes the deeper study of a malicious sample that an investigation hands off for detailed analysis.
- [eDiscovery Specialist](ediscovery-specialist.md): produces electronically stored information for litigation, a related discipline centered on legal process rather than technical investigation.
- [Forensics Manager](forensics-manager.md): oversees casework, standards, and the team, a direction an experienced analyst may grow toward.
