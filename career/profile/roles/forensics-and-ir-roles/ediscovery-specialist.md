# eDiscovery Specialist

Sitting at the intersection of law, technology, and data management, an **eDiscovery Specialist** manages the **electronically stored information (ESI)** that organizations must locate and produce during litigation, internal investigations, and regulatory inquiries. The work follows the stages described by the **Electronic Discovery Reference Model (EDRM)**: identification, preservation, collection, processing, review, analysis, and production. It is less about adversaries than about accuracy, completeness, and chain of custody under legal scrutiny, gathering the right records at scale and making them available to attorneys in a defensible, reproducible way.

## Responsibilities

- Scope matters with legal teams, then issue and track **legal holds** to suspend routine deletion.
- Identify the custodians and data sources in scope, from email and file shares to chat platforms, cloud collaboration suites, and mobile devices.
- Run forensically sound collections that preserve metadata, and load data into review platforms such as Relativity or comparable tools.
- Apply culling techniques such as deduplication, date filtering, keyword searching, and technology-assisted review to reduce large volumes to a manageable set.
- Coordinate attorney review workflows and manage privilege and responsiveness coding.
- Generate production sets in the formats and with the redactions that court or regulatory requirements demand, documenting every step.

## Skills and background

- **Technical foundations:** working knowledge of file systems, email architectures, metadata, and common enterprise data stores.
- **Tooling:** fluency in at least one review platform and its search and analytics features.
- **Legal framework:** understanding of rules of civil procedure, proportionality, spoliation risk, and the obligations that attach to a litigation hold.
- **Project management:** attention to detail and the discipline to keep large collections organized and on schedule.
- **Communication:** clear coordination with attorneys and with non-technical custodians, because errors in preservation or production carry serious legal consequences.

## Getting into the role

People typically enter from paralegal or legal support backgrounds, from IT and data administration, or from forensics. Many hold vendor certifications such as the Relativity credentials or the **Certified E-Discovery Specialist** designation offered by ACEDS. Early roles often center on processing, document review coordination, or project support.

## Career progression

Growth leads toward litigation support management, eDiscovery project lead, or in-house roles that own an organization's discovery program and its information governance policies. Some specialists move deeper into forensic collection, while others move toward governance, privacy, and compliance work as data volumes and regulatory demands expand.

## Related roles

- [Digital Forensics Analyst](digital-forensics-analyst.md): performs the technical acquisition of devices and reconstructs system activity, where the specialist focuses on legal process.
- [Incident Responder](incident-responder.md): generates records that later become relevant to litigation or regulatory reporting.
- [Forensics Manager](forensics-manager.md): shares tooling, evidence handling discipline, and chain of custody standards, though eDiscovery usually reports through legal rather than through forensics.
