# eDiscovery Specialist

An eDiscovery specialist manages the electronically stored information (ESI) that organizations must locate and produce during litigation, internal investigations, and regulatory inquiries. The role sits at the intersection of law, technology, and data management, and it follows the stages described by the Electronic Discovery Reference Model (EDRM): identification, preservation, collection, processing, review, analysis, and production. Where a digital forensics analyst focuses on reconstructing what happened on a system, the eDiscovery specialist is concerned with gathering the right records at scale and making them available to attorneys in a defensible, reproducible way. The work is less about adversaries and more about accuracy, completeness, and chain of custody under legal scrutiny.

Day to day, the specialist scopes matters with legal teams, issues and tracks legal holds to suspend routine deletion, and identifies the custodians and data sources in scope, from email and file shares to chat platforms, cloud collaboration suites, and mobile devices. They run forensically sound collections that preserve metadata, load data into review platforms such as Relativity or comparable tools, and apply culling techniques like deduplication, date filtering, keyword searching, and technology-assisted review to reduce large volumes to a manageable set. They coordinate attorney review workflows, manage privilege and responsiveness coding, and generate production sets in formats and with redactions that match court or regulatory requirements. Careful documentation accompanies every step so that the process can withstand challenge.

The role calls for a blend of technical and procedural skills. A specialist needs working knowledge of file systems, email architectures, metadata, and common enterprise data stores, along with fluency in at least one review platform and its search and analytics features. Equally important is an understanding of the legal framework, including rules of civil procedure, proportionality, spoliation risk, and the obligations that attach to a litigation hold. Attention to detail, strong project management, and clear communication with attorneys and non technical custodians distinguish effective practitioners, because errors in preservation or production can carry serious legal consequences for the organization.

People typically enter the field from paralegal or legal support backgrounds, from IT and data administration, or from forensics, and many hold vendor certifications such as the Relativity credentials or the Certified E-Discovery Specialist designation offered by ACEDS. Early roles often center on processing, document review coordination, or project support, and growth leads toward litigation support management, eDiscovery project lead, or in house roles that own an organization's discovery program and its information governance policies. Some specialists move deeper into forensic collection, while others move toward governance, privacy, and compliance work as data volumes and regulatory demands continue to expand.

Within a forensics and incident response organization, the eDiscovery specialist is a close neighbor to the digital forensics analyst, who may perform the technical acquisition of devices, and to incident responders, who generate records that later become relevant to litigation or regulatory reporting. The specialist rarely performs malware analysis or breach containment, and generally reports through legal or litigation support rather than through a forensics manager, though the two functions often share tooling, evidence handling discipline, and chain of custody standards.
