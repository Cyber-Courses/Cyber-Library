# CTI Manager

A CTI Manager, or Cyber Threat Intelligence Manager, leads the function responsible for understanding the adversaries, campaigns, and techniques that threaten an organization. The role sits at the point where analysis meets management. Rather than producing most intelligence firsthand, the manager sets the direction of the program, defines what the team collects and why, and ensures that the resulting insight reaches the people who can act on it. The position matters because threat intelligence is only valuable when it is prioritized, trusted, and connected to decisions, and that connection rarely happens without someone accountable for the whole pipeline from requirement to delivery.

Day to day, the work is a blend of program management, analytic quality control, and communication. A CTI Manager defines priority intelligence requirements with stakeholders such as the security operations center, incident response, vulnerability management, and senior leadership, then organizes the team's collection and analysis against those requirements. They review finished products for accuracy and clarity, manage relationships with intelligence vendors and information sharing communities, and oversee the platforms and feeds the team depends on. Much of the role involves briefing others, from a tactical update for defenders to a strategic assessment for executives or the board, and tailoring the message so that each audience understands what a given threat means for them. Budgeting, hiring, mentoring analysts, and measuring whether the program actually reduces risk round out the responsibilities.

The role calls for a foundation in intelligence analysis combined with the judgment to lead people and represent the function to the wider business. Strong candidates understand the intelligence lifecycle, common adversary frameworks such as MITRE ATT&CK, and how tactical, operational, and strategic intelligence differ in purpose and audience. Equally important are the softer capabilities: writing clearly, managing competing priorities, and speaking credibly to both engineers and executives. A working knowledge of security operations, incident response, and the organization's own risk posture helps the manager keep the program grounded in what the business needs rather than in interesting but irrelevant threat activity.

Most people reach this role after several years as a threat intelligence analyst or in a closely related discipline such as incident response or security operations, where they build the analytic instincts and domain knowledge the job depends on. Progression usually comes through taking ownership of a collection area, leading projects, and gradually mentoring more junior analysts before formally managing a team. From the CTI Manager position, common paths include director level leadership over a broader intelligence or detection organization, a move into overall security program management, or a specialized track as a principal analyst or advisor for organizations that prefer deep expertise over people management.

The CTI Manager relates to its neighboring roles as the person who directs and integrates their work rather than duplicating it. Threat Intelligence Analysts and Strategic Threat Analysts produce the tactical and strategic assessments the manager prioritizes and quality checks, Threat Researchers supply the deep technical investigation into malware and adversary tooling, and Dark Web Analysts contribute specialized collection from closed and criminal sources. The manager's distinct contribution is orchestration and accountability: deciding what the function should focus on, ensuring the output is sound and timely, and making certain that intelligence changes what the organization actually does to defend itself.
