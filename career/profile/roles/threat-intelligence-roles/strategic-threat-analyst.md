# Strategic Threat Analyst

Most of threat intelligence concerns the specific indicators and techniques seen in a given incident. A **Strategic Threat Analyst** works at a higher altitude, studying the broad forces shaping an organization's **threat landscape** and translating them into guidance leaders can use for long-range decisions. The concern is who is likely to target the organization over the coming months and years, why, and what that means for investment, posture, and risk. The defining aim is to connect developments in the wider world, from geopolitical tension to shifts in criminal economies, regulatory change, and the motivations of threat actors, to the choices an organization must make about where to spend its defensive effort.

## Responsibilities

- Gather and weigh information from a wide range of sources and assess the credibility and significance of what is found.
- Produce assessments describing how the threat environment is changing and the likely implications.
- Deliver briefings, forecasts, and reports aimed at senior stakeholders, framing conclusions in terms of **confidence and uncertainty** rather than false precision.
- Define and refine **intelligence requirements**, the questions the organization most needs answered, so analysis stays tied to real decisions.
- Keep work anchored to what the business values rather than drifting toward interesting but unused information.

## Skills and background

- **Reasoning:** strong analytical thinking and the ability to reason in terms of probability and evidence.
- **Method:** familiarity with structured analytic techniques that guard against bias and help reason through ambiguity.
- **Breadth:** awareness of geopolitics, economics, and the organization's own business, alongside a working understanding of how cyber threats operate even without examining malware or networks directly.
- **Judgment and discretion:** essential, since the audience is often executives and board members acting on the assessments.

## Getting into the role

People commonly enter after building a foundation elsewhere rather than starting here directly. Some arrive from tactical or operational threat intelligence work, having developed the subject-matter grounding and then moved toward broader, longer-horizon questions. Others come from intelligence analysis, international affairs, risk, or research, bringing analytic discipline that transfers well once paired with cyber domain knowledge. Recognized training in intelligence analysis and cyber threat intelligence can support this path, though a track record of assessments that proved useful carries particular weight.

## Career progression

Growth tends to move from contributing to assessments under guidance, toward owning intelligence requirements for a business area, shaping the organization's overall intelligence picture, and advising leadership directly.
