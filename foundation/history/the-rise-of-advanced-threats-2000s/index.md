# The Rise of Advanced Threats 2000s

If the 1990s introduced cybercrime, the 2000s industrialized it. Internet connectivity was now always on and widespread, which gave fast-moving threats a far larger population of targets. In 2003 the SQL Slammer worm spread across vulnerable servers in minutes, generating so much traffic that it disrupted networks worldwide. Its speed showed that an automated threat could affect global infrastructure faster than human operators could react.

Attackers also grew more patient and more organized. The 2008 Conficker worm infected millions of machines and quietly linked them together, resisting removal and demonstrating how a compromised population of computers could be coordinated at scale. This was the age of the botnet: large networks of hijacked machines controlled remotely and rented out for spam, fraud, and disruption. Security shifted from stopping single infections to disrupting entire criminal supply chains.

Data itself became the prize. The TJX breach disclosed in 2007 exposed tens of millions of payment card records and became one of the defining incidents of the decade, revealing how weaknesses in retail and wireless systems could translate directly into financial theft. It pushed payment security standards and breach disclosure into mainstream business conversation.

By the end of the 2000s, cybersecurity had become a boardroom concern. The era established the patterns, automation, scale, and monetized data, that still shape the threat landscape today.
