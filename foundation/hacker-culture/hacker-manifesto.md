# Hacker Manifesto

**The Hacker Manifesto** is a short essay written in 1986 by Loyd Blankenship under the handle The Mentor, properly titled "The Conscience of a Hacker." In fewer than a thousand words it argued that hacking was driven by curiosity rather than greed or malice, and that reaching into a system was a way to learn how the world really worked. It remains one of the most quoted texts in hacker culture, and also one of the most argued over.

## Written shortly after my arrest

The essay carries its own backstory in its first line: "The following was written shortly after my arrest." Blankenship dated it January 8, 1986, in the aftermath of being caught, as he later put it, in "a computer I shouldn't have been." Rather than a legal defense, he produced a piece of rhetoric aimed at the public.

It reached readers through Phrack, an underground electronic magazine passed between bulletin board systems (BBSes), the dial-up boards where most early hacker writing circulated. The manifesto ran as the third file of Phrack's seventh issue on September 25, 1986. Blankenship has said he wanted to push back against the image of hackers left by the 1983 film *WarGames*, in which a curious teenager nearly triggers a nuclear launch.

## The curiosity-and-access argument

The text is written as a sequence of short scenes, each ending with the adult world's verdict: "They're all alike." A bored student who already knows the lesson discovers a machine that "does what I want it to"; through the phone line he finds a community he knows "even if I've never met them." The argument then turns into an accusation, reaching the line most people remember: "My crime is that of curiosity."

Two beliefs sit under that line, and they are the core of what people mean by the hacker ethic:

- **Curiosity is a motive, not a cover story.** The drive to understand a system is treated as legitimate in itself.
- **Access is how you learn.** School served "baby food" while the network offered "steak," so reaching the machine was framed as the honest path to knowledge.

## Where a modern reader pushes back

The manifesto is a protest, not a code of conduct, which is why security practitioners read it critically today. The sharpest tension is that curiosity, however sincere, does not erase the harm an intrusion can cause.

| The essay's claim | How a modern reader answers |
|---|---|
| Curiosity justifies the act | Motive does not undo damage to real systems and people |
| Access is the only way to learn | Labs, capture-the-flag events, and authorized tests teach the same skills legally |
| "You call us criminals" is unfair | Authorization, not a hostile label, is what separates sanctioned testing from an uninvited intrusion; whether an act is a crime then turns on the conduct and the local law |

That last point is where the manifesto meets the later vocabulary of [white, black, and grey hats](white-black-and-grey-hats.md), which replaced motive with permission as the dividing line.

## The afterlife of a text file

A file meant for a few thousand modem users became a cultural fixture. It was reprinted across the early web, quoted in the 1995 film *Hackers*, and read aloud by Blankenship at a hacker conference in 2002. His own story also mattered: a 1990 Secret Service raid tied to his work helped prompt the founding of the Electronic Frontier Foundation.

Read now, the manifesto is a historical document, a window into how one generation saw itself. Its celebration of exploration connects to the [origins](origins.md) of hacking and to the [curiosity that drives innovation](curiosity-and-innovation.md), while its communal voice fed the broader [hacker ethic and subcultures](subcultures-and-values.md). The same impulse survives in lawful form today, as bug bounties, authorized testing, and responsible disclosure.
