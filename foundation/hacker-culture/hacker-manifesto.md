# Hacker Manifesto

**The Hacker Manifesto** is a short essay by Loyd Blankenship, a hacker who went by the handle The Mentor. Its real title is "The Conscience of a Hacker." In a few hundred words it argued that the drive behind hacking was not theft or vandalism but curiosity, and that gaining access to systems was a way of learning how the world actually works. It became one of the most quoted texts in [hacker culture](index.md).

## An arrest, a dial-up magazine, and a deadline

The essay opens with a one-line preface: "The following was written shortly after my arrest." Blankenship dated it January 8, 1986. At the time he was active in the hacker scene, including the group Legion of Doom, and he later summed up the arrest simply: he had been "in a computer I shouldn't have been."

The text reached readers months later. It ran as file 3 of 10 in Phrack, Volume One, Issue 7, released on September 25, 1986. Phrack was an underground magazine distributed through bulletin board systems (BBSes), the dial-up message boards where much early hacker writing circulated. Its editors needed material for the issue, and Blankenship has said he wanted to correct the public image of hackers left by the 1983 film *WarGames*: teenagers who might start a nuclear war by accident.

## Reading it passage by passage

The essay is built as a series of short scenes, each closed by the same dismissive refrain from the adult world: "They're all alike."

1. **The headlines.** It opens with newspaper stories about a teenager arrested for computer crime, and asks whether anyone ever looked "behind the eyes of the hacker."
2. **The classroom.** The narrator is bored by lessons he already understands and is written off as an underachiever or a cheat.
3. **The computer.** He finds a machine that "does what I want it to." If it fails, it is his own mistake, not because the machine dislikes him. The computer is presented as the first fair authority in his life.
4. **The board.** Through the phone line he reaches a BBS and a community: "I know everyone here... even if I've never met them."
5. **The indictment.** The tone turns into an accusation. "We explore... and you call us criminals." Society, he says, builds bombs and wages wars yet calls hackers the criminals. The climax is the line most people remember: "My crime is that of curiosity."

The final sentence turns the refrain back on the reader: "You may stop this individual, but you can't stop us all... after all, we're all alike."

## The claims, and the objections they meet today

| What the essay asserts | How it puts it | The objection a modern reader raises |
|---|---|---|
| Curiosity, not malice, drives hacking | "My crime is that of curiosity" | Motive does not undo harm: an intrusion can damage people and systems whatever the intent |
| Access is a way to learn | Schools served "baby food" while the network offered "steak" | Learning can happen without entering systems you do not own, in labs, capture-the-flag contests and authorized tests |
| Judge the act, not the label | Hackers are called criminals while wars and lies go unexamined | Comparing hacking to worse wrongs does not make it lawful |
| Hackers are one community | "We exist without skin color, without nationality, without religious bias" | The scene was far from that ideal, and "we" spoke for some members more than others |
| Paying for services is optional | Using a service "without paying for what could be dirt-cheap" | This is a justification for phone fraud, the part of the text that has aged worst |

The essay is a protest, not a program. It offers no rules for responsible behavior, which is why it reads best alongside the later debates over [white, black, and grey hats](white-black-and-grey-hats.md), where authorization, not motive, became the dividing line.

## From BBS file to cultural touchstone

The manifesto spread far beyond Phrack. It was reprinted across the early web, quoted in the 1995 film *Hackers*, and printed on posters and T-shirts. Blankenship read it aloud at the H2K2 (Hackers on Planet Earth) conference in 2002.

Its author's own story also fed into a larger fight. Blankenship went to work for Steve Jackson Games, and in 1990 the U.S. Secret Service raided the company and seized his manuscript for *GURPS Cyberpunk*. The raid, part of a wider crackdown on hackers, led to the founding of the Electronic Frontier Foundation (EFF), whose lawsuit helped establish that stored email deserves legal protection.

Today the essay is read as a historical document: a window into how a generation of hackers saw themselves, not a license to break into systems. Its celebration of exploration connects to the older [origins](origins.md) of hacking and to the [curiosity that drives innovation](curiosity-and-innovation.md). Its values fed the broader [hacker ethic and subcultures](subcultures-and-values.md), and modern security work channels the same impulse into lawful forms such as authorized testing, bug bounties, and responsible disclosure.
